How it works

What data sharing is Draft, needs review

v5, after supervisor review. Adds explicit framing of data sharing as who should have access to what information and which stakeholders need review. Benefits below now covers mechanism only; party-specific benefits moved to the Benefits by party tab.

Data sharing is the process of sharing information between agencies to streamline eligibility determinations and other procedures. There are a few legal documents and agreements that work to enable a program to share data with the right parties: client consents, also called releases of information, vendor agreements, subrecipient agreements, use agreements, and data sharing agreements. These work together, shaped by the scope negotiated with each partner.

More specifically, data sharing sets who should have access to what information, and which stakeholders need to be reviewed and evaluated before that access is granted. The sections below walk through both: first the parties and agreements involved, then the specific documents each one requires.

Moving toward a more integrated approach, the kind of change this playbook is written for, usually means revisiting those relationships and the agreements that support them. Done well, that reduces the burden on clients.

The Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program (RWHAP) must be the payer of last resort. Confirming a client's other coverage is a legal requirement. The Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) also stresses putting data-sharing agreements in place to streamline operations and reduce client burden. [source: PCN 21-02]

Scope Draft, needs review

Before drafting any agreement, a jurisdiction has to work out its scope:

  • Which programs are involved: Ryan White, and often Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) and Housing Opportunities for Persons With AIDS (HOPWA) alongside it.
  • Which departments and agencies are involved: the health department itself, other units such as HIV surveillance, contracted and non-contracted providers, vendors, and outside agencies such as Medicaid.
  • Which data systems will be used for eligibility determinations and other source data.

These parties and agreements are covered in the sections below: parties are the health department, funded and unfunded providers, vendors, and other agencies; agreements are what connect them.

What this makes possible Draft, needs review

Two things come from getting these agreements right.

Streamlining operations. When lab data or other source data feeds directly into the system, case managers don't need to pull that data by hand and re-enter it. Automated checks for income, Medicaid status, or residency cut the time case managers spend requesting and processing documentation.

Reducing barriers for clients. Today, a client typically brings in pay stubs, a driver's license, and proof of insurance every year, and again whenever something changes, because the program only learns about a change when the client reports it. Automated checks replace that with something closer to real time. Medicaid status, for example, can be checked monthly instead of annually, without the client bringing anything in. If a client's coverage changes mid-year, the system can reflect that on its own. Fewer documentation requests also means fewer clients losing eligibility simply because they missed a paperwork deadline, which supports retention in care.

A 2025 federal law requires states to redetermine Medicaid eligibility for expansion adults at least every six months instead of annually, and adds new work requirements starting in 2027. That's expected to make Medicaid coverage more episodic for some clients, on and off within a year, which makes automated, frequent checking more useful than an annual snapshot. [source: KFF, Tracking Implementation of the 2025 Reconciliation Law]

Party-specific benefits, including what a program gets from Medicaid data and what a client gets from broader consent, are covered in the Benefits by party tab.

Health department

Meeting the payer-of-last-resort requirement is the starting point: the Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program can only cover what other coverage doesn't, and reliable data sharing is what makes confirming that coverage possible at scale. [source: PCN 21-02]

From there, the gains are direct. Data from a partner like Medicaid means faster, cheaper eligibility checks than manual verification. It can help confirm a client's state residency, since Medicaid eligibility itself generally requires it. It can surface other care-related data that supports smoother transitions of care.

HRSA's Technical Expert Panel found that recipients using the flexibility in PCN 21-02 alongside better data access saw decreased staff time processing applications, decreased burden on clients, and quicker turnarounds. [source: HRSA TEP Executive Summary]

A few outcomes, sourced from real jurisdictions:

  • Cross-jurisdiction accuracy. Electronic data exchange between DC, Maryland, and Virginia cut the manual duplicate-review caseload by roughly three-quarters with Maryland and four-fifths with Virginia, and helped DC identify people who had moved out of the District rather than appearing to have disappeared from care. [source: JMIR Public Health & Surveillance, 2018] Figures still need sign-off. See .
  • Coordinated internal sharing. Where surveillance, prevention, and Ryan White functions sit under one office, internal sharing doesn't need a separate agreement, freeing staff to focus on the outside partnerships that do. [source: Louisiana Department of Health, Integrated HIV Prevention and Care Plan, 2022 to 2026] See .
  • Better research and outcome measurement. North Carolina's integrated hub lets the health department match surveillance, ADAP/Ryan White, and Medicaid data internally, and return to each partner only what it's entitled to see. [source: NASTAD, HIV Data Privacy and Confidentiality] See .

Still needs research: the benefit to the health department's own surveillance unit, specifically, if it's asked to share more internally. Not yet answered.

Client

A broader consent, covering the full network of providers and agencies rather than one relationship at a time, cuts how much documentation a client has to provide, and to how many people. It makes services easier to access, since any authorized provider can confirm eligibility without the client re-proving it. It supports better-coordinated care, since providers can see what care a client has or hasn't received.

Underlying all of it: the health department can't pay for a client's care until eligibility is confirmed, and confirming eligibility depends on this access. Client consent is what makes the payment side of the system work.

TEP participants also pointed to Medicaid and motor vehicle records as sources some jurisdictions have used to verify income and residency for clients who might otherwise be treated as lost to follow-up, meaning fewer clients fall out of care over documentation alone. [source: HRSA TEP Executive Summary]

Other agencies

This is the hardest category to standardize, and the least finished: each partner has its own legal authority and comfort level, so agreements get negotiated one partner at a time. Two open items:

  • Medicaid. What the program gets from Medicaid data is covered under Health department above. What Medicaid itself gets from sharing back, the case that would actually be made in a negotiation, still needs research or a direct conversation with a Medicaid partner.
  • Other health departments (surveillance matching). Same gap. Still needs research.

This matters for negotiation: no partner agrees to the cost and effort of a data-sharing agreement without a reason of their own, and right now that reason isn't drafted for either partner type.

Barriers Draft, needs review

v2, after supervisor review. Adds the "About this source" note below and substantially expands "Privacy concerns shape what gets shared and how." Other barriers are unchanged from v1.

About this source

In 2023, HRSA's HIV/AIDS Bureau convened a Technical Expert Panel (TEP) to study how RWHAP recipients were implementing PCN 21-02 and where they were running into trouble streamlining eligibility. The barriers below, unless noted otherwise, come directly from that panel's executive summary, a primary HRSA source. [source: HRSA TEP Executive Summary]

Establishing and maintaining data sharing agreements is hard. TEP participants identified access to and effective use of data, particularly through establishing and maintaining data-sharing agreements, as an ongoing challenge. [source: HRSA TEP Executive Summary]

Finding the right gatekeeper takes time. TEP participants noted that identifying who can grant access to needed data, and then negotiating the agreement itself, is time-consuming. [source: HRSA TEP Executive Summary]

Most states share only limited data with Medicaid. Independent research found that most states share only aggregate data with Medicaid, and none allow Medicaid direct access to the HIV surveillance database. [source: NASTAD, HIV Data Privacy and Confidentiality]

Privacy concerns shape what gets shared and how. Data can generally only be shared for a given client if that client has consented to it. Consent doesn't automatically extend across every system that holds a client's data.

State HIV surveillance systems, for example, hold records for everyone diagnosed with HIV in the state. RWHAP clients are one part of that group, and only some of them have consented to share their information outside the surveillance system. Before a state can share surveillance data with a Ryan White program, it has to filter the match down to only the clients who've actually consented to that sharing. The same logic applies to Medicaid data: many RWHAP clients also appear in Medicaid's records, but a program can only receive the data for clients who've consented. The rest of the matched set stays out of reach.

Flagged, not yet sourced: one figure from the review call needs a real source before it is usable here: the approximation that Ryan White clients make up roughly half of a given state HIV surveillance database. That number was offered as a verbal example during the call and still needs a citation before it appears on the site.

[source: HRSA TEP Executive Summary, for the general privacy-as-barrier framing; the consent-matching mechanism is not yet independently sourced beyond the review call]

Reconciling consent models across programs is slow. Programs vary in how they collect client consent to share data, and getting legal review to align differing consent language across partner programs takes time. A blanket consent model is easier to manage than a point-to-point model that requires reconsent every time a new agreement is signed, but switching models, or reconciling two programs that use different models, requires legal review up front. [source: RAND, Sharing and Integrating HIV Client Data Across Provider Organizations]

Trust has to be built. Data sharing depends on more than legal authority to share. Health departments that have used surveillance data to support HIV care describe laying groundwork by engaging stakeholders to build trust in how sensitive information will be used, alongside establishing the legal and governance structure. [source: Sweeney et al., "Shifting the Paradigm," The Milbank Quarterly, 2013]

The law, and an agency's own policies, can be the obstacle. Laws governing disclosure of health information were often written at different times for different diseases, and don't necessarily reflect how a health department needs to operate today. In New York City, separate historical laws on HIV and STDs meant that sharing surveillance data between two programs inside the same health department counted as an impermissible disclosure. [source: "Legal and Policy Barriers to Sharing Data Between Public Health Programs in New York City," PMC]

North Carolina Draft, needs review

Attached to: Data sharing agreement; Client consent form; Subrecipient agreement

North Carolina built an integrated data hub combining HIV surveillance, ADAP/Ryan White, and Medicaid data. The health department matches the data internally and returns to each partner only the information that partner is entitled to receive. Independent ten-state legal research identified North Carolina as the only state to have built this kind of hub; most other states share only aggregate data with Medicaid, and none allow external access to the surveillance database itself.

[source: NASTAD, HIV Data Privacy and Confidentiality]

District of Columbia, Maryland, and Virginia Draft, needs review

Attached to: Data sharing agreement; Other agencies party

The three jurisdictions' health departments signed HIV surveillance data sharing agreements in 2014 and began regular electronic data exchange in 2017, using a privacy-preserving matching tool external to the health departments. Electronic exchange of surveillance and lab data cut the manual duplicate-review caseload by roughly three-quarters between DC and Maryland and four-fifths between DC and Virginia, and helped DC identify people who had migrated out of the District. That out-migration, rather than a data error, had been inflating its count of people living with HIV.

[sources: JMIR Public Health & Surveillance, 2018; NASTAD, Cross-Jurisdictional Data Sharing]

Louisiana Draft, needs review

Attached to: Data sharing agreement; Health department party

Washington Draft, needs review

Attached to: Data sharing agreement; Contract, including BAA

Before 2020, Washington's health department managed HIV service data across separate systems: a state RWHAP database and a separate ADAP application system. Case managers had to check both to confirm eligibility, and creating new user accounts required a small group of "superusers," which created delay. Clients needing services from more than one RWHAP Part had to fill out a separate application for each.

Washington's health department moved to a single shared platform across RWHAP Parts and ADAP. Getting there required a request for proposals, a VPN for secure data exchange with health clinics, data sharing agreements with the agencies administering RWHAP Parts A and C and with Medicaid, and business associate agreements across RWHAP Parts.

The state's own account of the process emphasizes relationship-building as much as the technical work. Seattle-King County Public Health, the RWHAP Part A recipient, also receives Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) funding with its own eligibility criteria and didn't initially join the health department's planning. Once they did, the two teams worked out how to document HIV status in the same shared system for both RWHAP and EHE-funded services.

The transition took two and a half years. Training was a weak point at first: the vendor's own curriculum wasn't detailed enough, and training a small group of superusers to train everyone else didn't work well. Washington's health department ended up building its own training materials and videos instead.

The result is one client eligibility profile shared across every relevant agency. Washington's own framing is that Parts A and B are a reasonable place to start. Parts C and D, and EHE agencies, still need to be brought in.

This source names the specific platform Washington adopted, which is a vendor's own commercial product. This entry deliberately refers to it generically rather than by name, to avoid implying a commercial relationship. That naming decision is still open; revisit if the platform should be named.

[source: HRSA LEAP, "Case Study: Building Relationships to Help Synchronize RWHAP Eligibility Requirements in Washington State," March 2026]

Illinois Draft, needs review

Attached to: Data sharing agreement; Subrecipient agreement

Before PCN 21-02, Illinois required a full eligibility reapplication every six months. The state already used a shared data system across RWHAP Parts A, B, and B ADAP, but the paperwork wasn't unified: separate forms per Part, repeated data entry, and multiple signatures. One missing form could stall an entire application for weeks while the client came back in person to sign it.

After PCN 21-02, Illinois consolidated the paperwork into a single program agreement form, extended eligibility confirmation from six months to twelve, and added self-attestation at the twelve-month mark for clients with no changes to report. It also aligned each client's eligibility cycle with their birth month, so renewal dates were easier for clients to remember. Some renewal periods were temporarily stretched or shortened, from nine to eighteen months, to make that alignment work.

The rollout leaned on partners: webinars run with AIDS Foundation Chicago and the Midwest AIDS Training and Education Center (a RWHAP Part F recipient), offered live and recorded to reach more staff, plus separate sessions for community-based organizations and for clients themselves.

A program administrator reported the change cut staff workload roughly in half, and most applications now process within 24 hours.

Illinois entry: efficiency figures (workload cut roughly in half; most applications processed within 24 hours) come from an independent published source, but are specific efficiency claims and need the same sign-off as any other figure before use on the site. Same platform-naming note as Washington above: the source names a specific vendor product, referred to generically here.

Illinois has a data sharing agreement with eight regional consortia, each led by an agency such as AIDS Foundation Chicago. RWHAP Part A agencies in a region can request read-only access; some get full access if they're jointly funded through both Part A and Part B. Next up: piloting cross-Part streamlining, and exploring a data sharing agreement with the state's Department of Revenue to automate income verification.

[source: HRSA LEAP, "Streamlining Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program Part B ADAP Eligibility: Moving to 12-month Self Attestation in Illinois," July 2026]
